How the CEFA Emerging Engine Works

From ~4,000 small & micro-caps to a hand of fully-researched, honestly-tracked asymmetric bets — once a week.

This engine does the opposite job to the daily scan. Instead of finding the strongest, most-confirmed large-caps, it hunts the small names nobody is looking at yet — scoring each for the structural DNA of a historical 100-bagger. It is structured speculation, not prediction: most of these names will not become multibaggers, and the engine is built to say so out loud.

The math finds the shape of a multibagger cheaply; the AI writes the theory — and, as hard, the threats.

The Emerging Engine · Full detail

A different machine, on purpose

This is not the daily scan pointed at smaller stocks. It is built on the opposite philosophy — and that single choice explains everything that follows.

The reason for the split: a stock that lights up every confirmation signal is, by definition, already discovered — and no longer cheap. The multibagger math lives where the daily scan refuses to fish. It keeps its own screener, scoring, research desk and dashboard, and never borrows the daily engine’s conviction grader.

Stage 1 — Build the universe (the cheap end of the market)

A few thousand names → kept. No AI. Rebuilt periodically. The engine builds its own list from authoritative, stable sources:

  • The NASDAQ screener — every US-listed common stock across NYSE / NASDAQ / AMEX (~7,000 names) with market cap, sector and IPO year.
  • The recent-IPO calendar — the last three years of new listings, to catch fresh names the screener hasn’t fully tagged.

We keep only names valued between $50M and $10B. Below $50M is too illiquid to research responsibly; above $10B the “small base” that makes 10× math possible is already gone.

Stage 2 — The DNA screen: six traits of a historical multibagger

Every name → six 0–100 scores. Pure math. No AI. Decades of 100-baggers share a recognisable shape. Each trait “passes” at 60; a name passing 5 of 6 advances, 4 lands on a watch list.

Stage 2 — the six multibagger traits

#TraitWhat earns a high score
1Small baseA cap in the $200M–$2B sweet spot scores 100 (room to grow 10×). $100M–$200M → 80; $2B–$5B → 60; $5B–$10B → 30.
2Durable revenue growthA 3-year revenue growth rate (CAGR) ≥ 40% scores 95; ≥ 25% → 80; ≥ 15% → 50. Growth that’s decelerating is penalised — fast and slowing is a warning.
3Improving unit economicsHigh and/or rising gross and operating margins. A margin still negative but clearly improving scores well — the signature of a business nearing escape velocity.
4Large addressable marketA coarse structural prior from sector and size (tiny company, big growing sector = more runway). The AI analyst refines the real TAM later.
5Aligned owner-operatorsReal insider ownership: ≥ 20% held by insiders → 95; ≥ 10% → 80. Recent insider buying (SEC Form 4) tilts up; heavy selling tilts down. Skin in the game.
6Under-discoveredThe inverted signal. Fewer analysts = a higher score: 0–2 → 95, above ~7 → 20. This is what makes the engine the mirror image of the daily scan.
Why trait 5 was worth rebuilding. Insider-ownership data isn’t on the vendor’s free tier, so this trait once sat at its neutral default for every company — alignment was effectively switched off. It now reads live ownership data, so a founder-led company with 50% insider ownership finally scores like one, and a company insiders are quietly exiting is finally marked down.

Stage 3 — The multibagger score (caps that can only subtract)

The screened names → one 0–100 score → the top 30 advance. Rules + math. No AI. The six traits roll up with fixed weights that encode what actually compounds:

ComponentWeightWhat it captures
Growth quality30%Real, durable revenue growth — the engine of the thesis
Reinvestment runway25%Room and economics to redeploy capital at high returns
Unit economics20%Margins that work, or are clearly on their way
Alignment15%Owner-operators betting alongside you
Discovery gap10%How undiscovered it still is

Then — exactly as in the daily grader — the risk checks can only move a score down:

  • Cash-burning, under a year of runway, not yet profitable → capped at 50 (dilution risk).
  • Debt-to-equity above 2.0 → capped at 40 (too levered for a long, bumpy road).
  • Revenue decelerating → capped at 50.

Accounting red flags (restatements, auditor changes, going-concern language) are treated as disqualifiers and surfaced in the deep-research stage’s “what kills it” analysis. The same credibility guarantee as the daily scan applies throughout: nothing can talk a score up — only evidence of quality lifts it, and known risks can only hold it back. The top 30 by capped score advance to deep research.

Stage 4 — The deep thesis: balanced, closed-context, verify-or-drop

The top 30 → one full thesis each. This is the AI work. An AI analyst writes a deliberately balanced dossier under the same anti-hallucination discipline as the daily desk: a closed data section it may not stray from, every specific number tagged to the exact data point it came from. Nine parts:

Two rules make this trustworthy, not promotional

① The rationale must verify, or the name is dropped. A deterministic checker confirms every number in the selection rationale traces to real data. If it can’t be verified, the name is removed from the run entirely — we never show a name we can’t justify. Every other section that fails isn’t faked: it’s replaced with an honest line naming exactly what couldn’t be confirmed (e.g. “No analyst coverage and few filings — moat not verifiable from connected sources.”). For these under-covered names, thin data is the norm, and the engine says “we don’t know” instead of inventing.

② Hype is mechanically banned. Phrases like “to the moon,” “guaranteed,” “next Nvidia,” “no-brainer,” and “easy 10x” cause the entire thesis to be discarded and rewritten. The voice is plain, balanced and risk-forward — the opposite of a stock-promotion newsletter.

Stage 5 — The watchlist (tiers, graduation, honest retirement)

Verified theses → a persistent, tiered watchlist. Rules. No AI. A name joins only with a complete, fact-checked dossier (when several theses exist for one name, the engine keeps the best-verified one, not the most recent if it’s weaker). Tier 1 (higher conviction) and Tier 2 (promising) always join; Tier 3 (speculative) joins only if its score clears 60. Prices, returns, peak gains and drawdowns refresh weekly. Two lifecycle rules keep it clean: a name whose market cap crosses $10B “graduates” out into the daily pipeline (it’s no longer small), and a name whose thesis breaks is archived, not quietly deleted.

Stage 6 — Honest outcome tracking (including the failures)

Every name ever added → a permanent return record. Pure math. For winners, losers and the dead alike, we keep a long-tail record and report engine-level statistics plainly: the share that hit 2× / 5× / 10× (with milestone dates), the share archived as broken, the average current return, and the median time to double for the names that doubled. A strategy that expects most names to fail has no credibility unless it shows the failures — so it does.

The risk framing isn’t a footnote — it’s the product. Every report and the dashboard carry it: “Emerging candidates are speculative, long-horizon, high-risk positions. Most will not become multibaggers. Small bets, long horizons, expect many failures and a few large winners. For licensed-advisor review — not investment advice.” The winners can return many multiples; the losers can only ever return −100%. The engine is sized around that asymmetry and never pretends otherwise.

Why this is the moat

  1. It fishes where confirmation-based screeners can’t. By rewarding obscurity it surfaces names before the crowd — the only place asymmetric returns live.
  2. The same anti-hallucination spine as the daily desk — closed context, every number checked, the core rationale verified-or-dropped. Where fabrication is easiest, it refuses to guess.
  3. “What kills it” is a deliverable, not a disclaimer — the failure case is as rigorous as the upside.
  4. Risk can only subtract — cash-burn, leverage, deceleration and accounting flags can only cap or exclude.
  5. It keeps score honestly, forever — every name it ever touched, hit-rate and failures published.
