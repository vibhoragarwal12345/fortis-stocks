The Commodities Engine · Full detail

The one idea: two clocks, never crossed

The classic way to get commodities wrong is to mix the horizons — to let a one-week trading observation bleed into a multi-year thesis, or vice versa. They are different questions, for different readers. So the engine refuses to blend them.

Tactical readStructural read
Horizon~1 weekMultiple quarters to years
Written forTradersInvestors
Built fromTechnicals, positioning, curve, the latest inventory surprise, seasonalitySupply/demand balance, macro drivers, long trends, inventory levels vs the 5-year norm
CarriesTimingNo timing information at all
Its rule“Don’t act on this with long-horizon capital”“Don’t trade off this read”

The two reads are assembled from two disjoint sets of data and written by two independent AI passes — the tactical writer never sees the structural framing, and vice versa. A horizon can’t leak by construction, and a separate AI auditor checks for leakage anyway.

Rather than ship a hollow tile beside the others, we pulled it — and we’ll only re-add a commodity when we can research it honestly. That decision is the whole philosophy in miniature.

Every figure carries a provenance tag — verified from a named live source, estimated (a statistical simulation), or unverified. Where a source is silent or paid-gated, the figure is marked unverified or left out — never guessed, never filled in. Label the gaps; don’t fill them.

The zero-drift reference bands (honest levels, not forecasts)

Layer 1 produces price reference bands — with a discipline worth spelling out, because it’s where most “price target” math quietly lies. For each commodity we run a 10,000-path Monte Carlo simulation, the return distribution fitted to the commodity’s own recent behaviour (fat tails included). It yields three levels on each clock — a lower band (5th percentile), a midpoint (50th), and an upper band (95th) — over a one-week (tactical) and a one-year (structural) horizon.

The crucial move: we zero out the drift. A naïve simulation extrapolates the recent trend forward — which over a year turned a two-year gold rally into a “+69% median” band: momentum dressed up as a statistical forecast. Zeroing the drift makes the bands describe volatility only — the range where price could plausibly sit given how it actually moves — with no directional view baked in. Direction belongs in the supply/demand and macro reads, not smuggled into a number. The bands are labelled “statistical reference levels — not forecasts, not targets,” and being pure math they exist even when a data source is down.

Step 3 — The narrative: the one AI layer, on a short leash

Two reads per commodity — the only place AI is used, and it’s fenced in. An AI writer turns the six layers into two plain-English narratives under the same closed-context discipline as the stock desk, and then some:

  • It sees only a data section of named keys computed this run — no outside knowledge, no remembered prices. Every number must be tagged to the exact data point it came from.
  • Every draft must clear three gates, in order:
    1. Not truncated — the text must end on a finished sentence (a cut-off read still fails).
    2. Fact-check ≥ 0.85 — a deterministic checker confirms every tagged number traces to a real data key and matches it.
    3. Critic audit — a second AI model checks for three things: a claim the data doesn’t support, any advice language (“buy / sell / hold / should”), and horizon leakage.
  • Each failure feeds its findings back into the next try. After three attempts the read is discarded and reported absent — never backfilled from the model’s memory. An honest blank beats a confident guess.

Because the tactical and structural narratives are two separate calls on two disjoint data sets, a horizon can fail its check on its own — and one read being absent never fabricates the other.

Step 4 — The cross-link: what commodities tell the stock side

Three structural signals. Deterministic, no AI, no new data. Copper, gold and oil are read-outs on the whole economy, so the engine distils three clean signals for the stock side’s macro analysis:

SignalFromWhat it reads
Industrial / growthCopper trendCopper demand is industrial production — a global-growth proxy (tailwind / headwind)
Risk appetiteGold trendA sustained gold bid is hedge / haven demand — a risk-off tilt
Inflation impulseOil (WTI) trendCrude feeds headline inflation with a short lag

Three rules keep this clean: the signals are structural by construction (50/200-day trend, not day moves); they are purely advisory — they colour the stock macro agent’s reasoning, never override its thresholds; and a missing or stale scan degrades to “no signal,” never a guess (stale after five days). Neither side imports the other’s code.

Cadence — a daily tactical pulse, a weekly structural refresh

Each weekday a lightweight tactical run refreshes the one-week read. Once a week (Monday) a full run regenerates everything, including the structural read — carried forward through the daily runs until the next Monday. The fundamentals behind it (EIA weekly, CFTC weekly, USDA/IMF monthly) don’t change fast enough to recompute intraday, so we don’t.

Research intelligence, not advice. The standing disclaimer is unmissable: commodities carry higher risk than equities (leverage, volatility, geopolitical shocks); all reference levels are statistical, not forecasts; data is delayed and partially incomplete, with gaps labelled, never filled; and a qualified human must review any decision made downstream.

Why this is the moat

  1. Two clocks, never crossed — separating the trade from the thesis architecturally stops a one-week signal masquerading as a multi-year view.
  2. Verify-or-label, never fabricate — every number tagged to a live source; gaps labelled; the AI may cite only data we have; a read that can’t clear three gates is dropped.
  3. Zero-drift honesty on the levels — bands describe volatility, not a disguised momentum forecast.
  4. A real cross-asset signal — copper, gold and oil feed the stock engine’s macro read through one clean, structural, deterministic interface.
  5. Risk-first by construction — every read carries its horizon warning and the reminder that commodities are riskier than equities.
